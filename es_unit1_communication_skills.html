Unit 1 — Overview
Communication Skills
Communication is a two-way process through which information or messages are exchanged between individuals using language, symbols, signs or behaviour. Effective communication skills help us communicate the message correctly, precisely and completely. Lack of communication skills can result in confusion, frustration, wasted effort and missed opportunities.

Four Key Skills

  • Listening
  • Speaking
  • Reading
  • Writing

Elements of Communication

  • Sender (encodes message)
  • Channel (medium)
  • Receiver (decodes message)
  • Feedback (confirms understanding)

Ideal Message Qualities

  • Clear — conveys exact meaning
  • Concise — appropriately brief
  • Accurate — factually correct & verifiable

What Makes a Message Clear, Concise & Accurate?

A clear statement conveys the exact message you are trying to send. Compare: "Please allow me to reach office at 11 AM on Tuesday" (clear — time is specified) vs "Please allow me to reach late" (vague).

A concise statement is appropriately brief and to the point — avoid adding information that belongs to a different forum or time.

An accurate statement is factual and verifiable. Compare: "50 per cent of the books have been completed" (accurate — measurable) vs "most of the books have been completed" (vague).

Session 1
Active Listening
Active listening is an art that comprises a desire to comprehend as well as offer support and empathy to the speaker. It can affect your job effectiveness, the quality of your relationships, and your overall well-being. Active listening allows you to understand problems and collaborate to develop solutions.

Why Listening Matters

📡

Obtain Information

Listening attentively helps gather accurate, complete information from others.

🤝

Build Relationships

Active listening builds and maintains strong personal and professional relationships.

⚡

Resolve Conflicts

Listening carefully helps understand all perspectives and work towards resolution.

🎓

Learn & Enjoy

We listen to enjoy, to learn, and to understand — attentive listening is the foundation of all learning.

Note: Without the ability to listen attentively, messages can be easily misunderstood, communication breaks down, and the sender of the message can become frustrated or irritated.

Factors That Affect Active Listening

Eye Contact

  • A form of body language
  • Signals "I am listening to you"
  • Avoiding eye contact implies disinterest

Gestures

  • Indicate to speaker if you are listening
  • Keep hands and feet still while talking
  • Open body language shows attentiveness

Avoiding Distractions

  • Physically remove distractions
  • Reduce ringtone / switch off phone in meetings
  • Avoid glancing at wristwatch frequently

Giving Feedback

  • Feedback can be positive or negative
  • Always be polite so the person is not hurt
  • Confirm understanding with relevant responses

5 Stages of Active Listening

1
Receiving

Listening attentively to the speaker — giving full, undivided attention.

2
Understanding

An informed agreement about what is being communicated — comprehending the message.

3
Remembering

Retrieving or recalling information — the retrieval of some information from the past.

4
Evaluating

Judging the value, quantity, importance and amount of what was said.

5
Responding

Saying or doing something as a meaningful response to what was communicated.

The RESPECT Model

Use the acronym RESPECT to ensure active listening in any situation:

R
Remove Distractions

Reduce the volume of television, radio or mobile phone while talking to a person.

E
Eye Contact

Look at the speaker while listening — it signals genuine attention.

S
Show You're Listening

Use gestures, nods, and open body language to show attentiveness to the speaker.

P
Pay Attention

Focus entirely on what the speaker is saying — not on planning your reply.

E
Empathise

Share the feelings of the speaker — imagine what it would be like to be in their situation.

C
Clarify Doubts

Ask polite, relevant questions to clarify any doubts or misunderstandings.

T
Tune to Timing

Wait for the speaker to finish before responding — never interrupt.

Barriers to Active Listening & How to Overcome Them

BarrierHow It Becomes a BarrierHow to Overcome It

Parts of Speech
In any language, parts of speech are categories of words based on their function within a sentence. They are the 'building blocks' of a language. A sentence always begins with a capital letter and ends with a full stop, question mark, or exclamation mark.

Sentence vs Phrase

A sentence is a group of words that communicates a complete meaning, thought or action. Example: Raju goes to school.

A phrase is a group of words that does NOT make complete sense. Example: Raju goes.

Same Word, Different Parts of Speech

Will you book a movie for 3 pm? — 'Book' is used as a verb.
I need to find my English book. — 'Book' is a noun.

His total cricket score was 145. — 'Total' is an adjective.
That is totally awesome. — 'Totally' is an adverb.

She is always watching a football match. — 'Football' is an adjective.
She got a new football for her birthday. — 'Football' is a noun.

Kapil promised his father he will be a good boy. — 'Promised' is a verb.
Kapil was unable to keep the promise. — 'Promise' is a noun.

Supporting Parts of Speech

Along with the main parts of speech, there are connecting words needed to join words, phrases, clauses or sentences. These are called supporting parts of speech.

📰

Articles

'a', 'an', 'the' — used before nouns. Use an before vowel sounds, a before consonant sounds, the for specific nouns. Example: a book, an apple, an umbrella, the sun.

🔗

Conjunctions

Join two nouns, phrases or sentences. Common: 'and', 'or', 'but'. Example: Sheela and I went to the market. Do you want oranges or apples?

📍

Prepositions

Connect words and show relationships. Answer 'where', 'when', 'how'. Common: 'on', 'at', 'under', 'in'. Example: The cat is on the roof. I live in Delhi.

❗

Interjections

Express strong emotions — happiness, surprise, anger or pain. Always have an exclamation mark. Example: Wow! Oh! Oh no! Thanks! Help!

Writing Sentences
A sentence is a group of words which together expresses a complete idea that has meaning. A sentence is formed by putting together a group of words in sequence so that it is understood by others. Every sentence has a subject and a verb; most also have an object.

Simple vs Complex Sentences

Simple Sentence

  • Has only one subject and one predicate
  • Contains only one finite verb
  • Example: Nisha sells a laptop.

Complex Sentence

  • Consists of two or more coordinate clauses
  • Joined by a coordinating conjunction
  • Example: She packed the product and sent it.

Direct & Indirect Objects
The object in a sentence can be either direct or indirect. Understanding the difference helps you construct precise, meaningful sentences.

Direct Object

A direct object is directly 'acted on' by the verb. It answers the question "What?"

Example: Nisha sells a laptop. → What does Nisha sell? → a laptop (direct object).

Indirect Object

An indirect object answers the questions "To whom?" or "For whom?" Some sentences only have direct objects; some have both direct and indirect objects.

Example: Abdul gave a gift to his mother.
Verb: gave → What did Abdul give? → a gift (direct object) → To whom? → his mother (indirect object).

Active & Passive Voice
Sentences can be classified based on whether the subject does the action or receives the action. This gives us active and passive voice.

Active Voice

Sentences where the subject does the action are in active voice.

Passive Voice

Sentences where the subject receives the action are in passive voice.

Compare:
1. Sanjay broke the glass. → Subject (Sanjay) does the action → Active voice
2. The glass was broken by Sanjay. → Subject (the glass) receives the action → Passive voice
Active VoicePassive Voice

Key Rule: In active voice, the subject does the action. In passive voice, the subject receives the action.
Session 3 — Continued
Types of Sentences
There are mainly four types of sentences — declarative, interrogative, exclamatory, and imperative. Each has a different purpose and ends with a specific punctuation mark.
📢

Declarative (Statement)

Provides information or states a fact. Most common type. Ends with a full stop (.).
"I go to office."
"It is very cold."
"I completed my project."

❓

Interrogative (Question)

Asks a question. Ends with a question mark (?).
"Did you go to office?"
"How is the weather?"
"Did you complete your project?"

😮

Exclamatory (Emotion)

Expresses strong emotion — joy, sadness, fear or wonder. Ends with an exclamation mark (!).
"Oh, it's very cold!"
"I completed my project!"
"No way! I don't want a tattoo!"

👉

Imperative (Order)

Shows an order, command, request or advice. Can end with a full stop or exclamation mark (. or !).
"Wear your sweater."
"Complete your project."
"Get out of the bed immediately!"

Writing Paragraphs
A group of sentences forms a paragraph. All sentences in a paragraph must share a common idea. When you want to write about a different idea, start a new paragraph.

How to Write a Good Paragraph

When writing a paragraph, ensure all sentences relate to the same central idea. For example, if you are writing about your school, the first paragraph can contain sentences about the name, location, size and other details. In the next paragraph, you can describe what you like about it.

Example paragraph:

I go to Government Higher Secondary School, Balachadi. It is not a very big school but has many good teachers. There are about 100 students in my school. My school is on the main road, very close to the city railway station.

I love going to school and learning about new things. My school has a small playground, where I play cricket with my friends after the classes are over. It has a library also. I love my school.
Tip: Practice writing paragraphs on topics you know — your school, a favourite hobby, or a career you aspire to. Focus on using all four sentence types correctly and ensuring all sentences in a paragraph share a single common idea.

Short Answer Questions
Q9. What are the three qualities of an ideal message? Give an example of each.
An ideal message should be: (1) Clear — conveys the exact meaning, e.g., "Please allow me to reach office at 11 AM on Tuesday, 11 June 2019." (2) Concise — appropriately brief and to the point, avoiding unnecessary information. The manager's reply "Alright, you may come late" is concise. (3) Accurate — factually correct and verifiable, e.g., "50 per cent of the books have been completed" (measurable) rather than "most of the books."
Q10. What is the RESPECT model? Name all seven steps.
RESPECT is an acronym to ensure active listening: R — Remove distractions (reduce TV/phone volume). E — Eye contact (look at the speaker). S — Show you are listening (through gestures). P — Pay attention (focus on what is being said). E — Empathise (share the feelings of the speaker). C — Clarify doubts (ask questions). T — Tune to timing (wait for speaker to finish before responding).
Q11. Write one sentence each for declarative, interrogative, exclamatory and imperative types.
Declarative: I completed my project on time.
Interrogative: Did you complete your project?
Exclamatory: I completed my project!
Imperative: Complete your project today.
Q12. Explain the difference between a direct object and an indirect object with an example.
A direct object is the person or thing that directly receives the action of the verb. It answers the question "What?". An indirect object answers "To whom?" or "For whom?". Example: "Abdul gave a gift to his mother." — Verb: gave. What did he give? → a gift (direct object). To whom? → his mother (indirect object).
Q13. What are the four barriers to active listening and how can they be overcome?
1. Being pre-occupied — Do not let emotions take over; keep phones away. 2. Noise and visual distractions — Create a conducive environment. 3. Past experiences/mindset — Avoid biases; be objective. 4. Personal factors — Allow the other person to finish speaking, then respond.
